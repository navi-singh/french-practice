# Chapter 2 Lessons: Être, Avoir, Subject Pronouns, and Negation

This chapter gives you the two most important verbs in French and the tools to make any sentence negative. By the end, you should be able to choose the right subject pronoun for any situation, conjugate être and avoir from memory, use each verb's fixed expressions, use `il y a`, and negate any of these structures correctly.

## Learning objectives

You will learn to:

1. choose the correct subject pronoun, including the `tu`/`vous` distinction and the uses of `on`;
2. conjugate and use `être` for identity, description, nationality, profession, and location;
3. recognize and use common fixed expressions built on `être`;
4. conjugate and use `avoir`, including idioms where English uses "to be";
5. use `il y a` to say what exists somewhere;
6. form negative sentences with `ne...pas` and apply the `de` rule after a negated `avoir`.

## Prerequisite quick check

Before beginning, make sure you can do the following from Chapter 1:

- give the four forms of "the" (`le`, `la`, `l'`, `les`) and the four forms of "a/some" (`un`, `une`, `des`);
- make an adjective agree in gender and number, for example `content` -> `contente` -> `contents` -> `contentes`.

If either skill feels shaky, review Chapter 1 briefly, because this chapter's example sentences use both articles and adjective agreement constantly.

## How to study this chapter

For each unit, read the explanation, say every French example aloud, cover the English and test comprehension, then cover the French and write it from memory. Complete the substitution prompts before moving to the next unit.

## Unit 1: Subject Pronouns

### Core idea

Every French sentence with a conjugated verb needs a subject: a noun or a subject pronoun. The subject pronoun tells you which verb ending to use.

| Person | Pronoun | English |
|---|---|---|
| 1st singular | `je` (`j'` before a vowel sound) | I |
| 2nd singular, familiar | `tu` | you (one person, informal) |
| 3rd singular, masculine | `il` | he/it |
| 3rd singular, feminine | `elle` | she/it |
| 3rd singular, indefinite | `on` | one/we/people (informal "we") |
| 1st plural | `nous` | we |
| 2nd plural or formal singular | `vous` | you (plural, or formal singular) |
| 3rd plural, masculine/mixed | `ils` | they |
| 3rd plural, feminine | `elles` | they (all feminine) |

### Tu versus vous

- Use `tu` with one person you know well, a family member, a child, or a peer in an informal context.
- Use `vous` with one person you do not know well, someone older, a person in authority, or any group of people, informal or formal.
- When in doubt with a single adult you have just met, choose `vous`. Native speakers rarely mind a too-formal `vous`, but an unwanted `tu` can sound rude.

### The many uses of on

`On` is grammatically 3rd person singular (it always takes the same verb form as `il`/`elle`), but its meaning is flexible:

- `On parle français ici.` — People speak French here. / French is spoken here.
- `On y va ?` — Shall we go?
- `Nous, on habite à Lyon.` — We live in Lyon. (very common spoken French: `on` replaces `nous`)

In everyday spoken French, `on` very often simply replaces `nous`. Written and formal French still prefer `nous`.

### Pronunciation note

`Ils` and `elles` sound identical before a consonant (`ils parlent` and `elles parlent` both start with `il-`/`el-` but the final `-s` is silent). Before a vowel sound, the `-s` links and is pronounced `z`: `ils arrivent` sounds like "il-z-arrivent." Listen for this liaison; it is often the only spoken clue that the subject is plural.

### Retrieval check

Choose the correct pronoun for each situation:

1. You are talking to your grandmother.
2. You are talking about your two female cousins together.
3. You are talking about yourself and your brother (a mixed pair).
4. You are asking your new coworker (whom you have just met) a question.

### Sentence-building practice

Build a French subject + verb skeleton for each situation above, without conjugating yet: for example, "you asking your grandmother" -> `tu` + verb. Say each pronoun aloud three times before moving on.

## Unit 2: Être (to be)

### Core idea

`Être` is the single most common French verb. Learn its six forms as one block, out loud, before you need them individually.

### Present tense of être

| Pronoun | Form | Pronoun | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| `je` | `suis` | `nous` | `sommes` |
| `tu` | `es` | `vous` | `êtes` |
| `il/elle/on` | `est` | `ils/elles` | `sont` |

### Main uses of être

`Être` links a subject to a description, identity, nationality, profession, or location.

- **Identity**: `C'est mon frère.` — This is my brother.
- **Description**: `Elle est fatiguée.` — She is tired.
- **Nationality**: `Nous sommes canadiens.` — We are Canadian.
- **Profession** (no article before an unmodified profession noun): `Il est ingénieur.` — He is an engineer.
  - Compare: `C'est un bon ingénieur.` — He is a good engineer. (An adjective before the profession requires `c'est un/une...`.)
- **Location**: `Vous êtes à Paris.` — You are in Paris.

### Common mistake

English speakers often add `un/une` before a profession after `être`: incorrect `*Il est un ingénieur.` The article disappears unless the profession noun is modified by an adjective, in which case you switch to `c'est un/une...`.

### Fixed expressions with être

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `être d'accord` | to agree |
| `être en retard` | to be late |
| `être en avance` | to be early |
| `être à l'heure` | to be on time |
| `être en train de + infinitive` | to be in the middle of doing something |
| `être content(e)/heureux(-se) de` | to be happy about |

- `Je suis d'accord avec toi.` — I agree with you.
- `Nous sommes en train de travailler.` — We are in the middle of working.
- `Elle est en retard pour la réunion.` — She is late for the meeting.

### Sentence-building practice

Complete each frame with a different subject and a true or invented detail:

1. `___ suis ___.` (a nationality or profession)
2. `___ est en train de ___.` (an -er verb infinitive)
3. `___ sommes d'accord avec ___.`

Then read all three aloud and rewrite them from memory.

## Unit 3: Avoir (to have) and il y a

### Core idea

`Avoir` translates literally as "to have," but French uses it in many situations where English uses "to be." Learning avoir's idioms as fixed chunks avoids many literal-translation mistakes.

### Present tense of avoir

| Pronoun | Form | Pronoun | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| `je` | `ai` | `nous` | `avons` |
| `tu` | `as` | `vous` | `avez` |
| `il/elle/on` | `a` | `ils/elles` | `ont` |

### Expressions with avoir

| French | Literal meaning | Natural English |
|---|---|---|
| `avoir ___ ans` | to have ___ years | to be ___ years old |
| `avoir faim` | to have hunger | to be hungry |
| `avoir soif` | to have thirst | to be thirsty |
| `avoir chaud` | to have heat | to be hot |
| `avoir froid` | to have cold | to be cold |
| `avoir raison` | to have reason | to be right |
| `avoir tort` | to have wrong | to be wrong |
| `avoir peur de` | to have fear of | to be afraid of |
| `avoir besoin de` | to have need of | to need |
| `avoir envie de` | to have desire of | to feel like/want |
| `avoir de la chance` | to have luck | to be lucky |

- `J'ai vingt-cinq ans.` — I am twenty-five years old.
- `Tu as froid ? Prends une veste.` — Are you cold? Take a jacket.
- `Elle a besoin d'un stylo.` — She needs a pen.
- `Nous avons envie de sortir ce soir.` — We feel like going out tonight.

### Common mistake

English speakers often translate age with `être`: incorrect `*Je suis vingt-cinq ans.` French always uses `avoir` for age: `J'ai vingt-cinq ans.`

### Il y a: there is/there are

`Il y a` is invariable — it never changes for number. Use it to state that something exists.

- `Il y a une pharmacie au coin de la rue.` — There is a pharmacy on the corner.
- `Il y a des enfants dans le jardin.` — There are children in the garden.
- `Il y a un problème.` — There is a problem.

### Sentence-building practice

Write four sentences using four different `avoir` expressions from the table, then one sentence with `il y a` describing your home or neighborhood. Read them aloud, cover them, and rewrite from memory.

## Unit 4: Negation with ne...pas

### Core idea

To make a sentence negative, place `ne` before the conjugated verb and `pas` immediately after it. Before a vowel sound, `ne` shortens to `n'`.

### Pattern

| Affirmative | Negative |
|---|---|
| `Je suis prêt.` | `Je ne suis pas prêt.` |
| `Elle a un chat.` | `Elle n'a pas de chat.` |
| `Il y a du café.` | `Il n'y a pas de café.` |

- `Nous ne sommes pas en retard.` — We are not late.
- `Ils n'ont pas faim maintenant.` — They are not hungry right now.
- `Il n'y a pas de problème.` — There is no problem.

### The de rule after a negated avoir

When a direct object introduced by an indefinite article (`un`, `une`, `des`) or a partitive article (`du`, `de la`, `de l'`, covered in Chapter 9) follows a negated `avoir` or `il y a`, that article normally becomes simply `de` (`d'` before a vowel sound). This rule does **not** apply after a negated `être`, and it does not change a *definite* article (`le`, `la`, `les`).

- `J'ai une voiture.` -> `Je n'ai pas de voiture.` — I have a car. -> I do not have a car.
- `Il y a des chaises.` -> `Il n'y a pas de chaises.` — There are chairs. -> There are no chairs.
- but: `C'est une voiture.` -> `Ce n'est pas une voiture.` — It is a car. -> It is not a car. (`être`, so the article stays)
- and: `J'aime le café.` -> `Je n'aime pas le café.` — I like coffee. -> I do not like coffee. (definite article stays)

### Common mistakes to avoid

1. **Forgetting the second negation word.** `ne` alone is not enough in standard written French: write `Je ne sais pas`, not `*Je sais pas` (that form exists only in very casual speech).
2. **Applying the de rule after être.** `Ce n'est pas un problème`, not `*Ce n'est pas de problème`.
3. **Applying the de rule to a definite article.** `Elle n'aime pas les légumes`, not `*Elle n'aime pas de légumes`, because `les légumes` here is a definite article expressing a general category, not an indefinite quantity.
4. **Forgetting `n'` before a vowel sound.** Write `Il n'y a pas`, not `*Il ne y a pas`.

### Sentence-building practice

Turn each sentence negative, applying the `de` rule where it belongs:

1. `J'ai un ordinateur.`
2. `Nous sommes fatigués.`
3. `Il y a une solution.`
4. `Tu aimes le sport.`

## Cumulative sentence-usage practice

These sentences combine subject pronouns, `être`, `avoir`, `il y a`, and negation together, the way you will actually need them in conversation. Read each sentence aloud twice, cover it, and write it from memory.

1. `Je suis étudiant, mais ma sœur est ingénieure.` — I am a student, but my sister is an engineer.
2. `Nous avons deux chambres et un petit jardin.` — We have two bedrooms and a small garden.
3. `Il y a une pharmacie près de la maison.` — There is a pharmacy near the house.
4. `Mes voisins ne sont pas bruyants.` — My neighbors are not noisy.
5. `On n'a pas de café, alors on a soif.` — We don't have coffee, so we're thirsty.
6. `Elle a vingt ans et elle est en train d'étudier le français.` — She is twenty years old and she is in the middle of studying French.
7. `Vous n'êtes pas en retard ; il est encore tôt.` — You are not late; it is still early.

### Notice and mark

For each sentence above, circle the conjugated verb (`être` or `avoir`) and underline the word or phrase that tells you the subject.

### Change the sentence

Rewrite sentences 1-4 twice each:

1. change the subject, person, or number;
2. change one meaningful detail (the profession, the number of rooms, the neighbor's trait).

Make every required change to the verb form, the article, and the negation.

### Make it personal

Write four new sentences about your own life using `être`, `avoir`, `il y a`, and one negative sentence. Read them aloud, check them, and rewrite only the parts you corrected.

## Mini-dialogue: meeting a new roommate

**Sophie :** `Bonjour ! Tu es la nouvelle coloc' ?` — Hi! Are you the new roommate?

**Amina :** `Oui, c'est moi. Je suis Amina, je suis étudiante en médecine.` — Yes, that's me. I'm Amina, I'm a medical student.

**Sophie :** `Enchantée. Moi, c'est Sophie. Tu as faim ? Il y a du pain et des fruits dans la cuisine.` — Nice to meet you. I'm Sophie. Are you hungry? There is bread and fruit in the kitchen.

**Amina :** `Non merci, je n'ai pas très faim. Mais j'ai soif !` — No thank you, I'm not very hungry. But I'm thirsty!

**Sophie :** `Il y a de l'eau et du jus dans le frigo. Tu n'es pas fatiguée après le voyage ?` — There is water and juice in the fridge. Aren't you tired after the trip?

**Amina :** `Un peu. Mais je suis contente d'être ici. Il n'y a pas de bruit dans la rue, c'est agréable.` — A little. But I'm happy to be here. There's no noise on the street, that's nice.

### Guided analysis

1. Find every form of `être` in the dialogue and identify which subject pronoun or noun controls it.
2. Find every form of `avoir` and match it to its meaning (possession, hunger, thirst, age, or an idiom).
3. Find two negative sentences and explain whether the `de` rule applied and why.
4. Locate `être en train de` and explain what action is in progress.
5. Rewrite Sophie's second line using `vous` instead of `tu`, making every necessary change.

## Connected reading: ma nouvelle vie à Montréal

`Je m'appelle Karim et je suis à Montréal depuis deux mois. Je suis étudiant et j'ai vingt-trois ans. Mon appartement n'est pas très grand, mais il y a une belle vue sur le fleuve. Le matin, j'ai souvent faim, alors je mange un fruit et je bois un café. Mes colocataires sont sympathiques : Léa est québécoise et elle est en train d'apprendre l'espagnol ; Tom est américain et il a toujours des histoires intéressantes. On n'est jamais seuls ici, et je n'ai pas peur de parler français, même si je fais des erreurs.`

— My name is Karim and I have been in Montreal for two months. I am a student and I am twenty-three years old. My apartment is not very big, but there is a beautiful view of the river. In the morning, I am often hungry, so I eat a fruit and drink a coffee. My roommates are nice: Léa is from Quebec and she is in the process of learning Spanish; Tom is American and he always has interesting stories. We are never alone here, and I am not afraid to speak French, even if I make mistakes.

3. Identify the two nationality adjectives and explain their agreement.
4. Rewrite the first three sentences replacing `Karim` with `Nadia` and making every needed change (pronouns, adjective agreement, verb forms).
5. Write three true sentences about your own living situation using the reading as a model.

## Chapter reference sheet

### Être and avoir, side by side

| Pronoun | être | avoir |
|---|---|---|
| `je` | `suis` | `ai` |
| `tu` | `es` | `as` |
| `il/elle/on` | `est` | `a` |
| `nous` | `sommes` | `avons` |
| `vous` | `êtes` | `avez` |
| `ils/elles` | `sont` | `ont` |

### Negation quick reference

| Structure | Rule |
|---|---|
| Any conjugated verb | `ne` (`n'`) + verb + `pas` |
| Indefinite/partitive object after negated `avoir` | article becomes `de`/`d'` |
| Definite article object | stays unchanged |
| Object after negated `être` | stays unchanged |

### Core habits

- Learn `être` and `avoir` as two complete six-form blocks, not as isolated forms.
- Memorize `avoir` idioms (age, hunger, thirst, needs, fear) as full phrases, not word-by-word translations.
- Say the negative frame `ne...pas` as a single unit surrounding the verb.
- Check every negated `avoir`/`il y a` sentence for the `de` rule before moving on.
