# Chapter 0 Exercises

Complete these with the lessons closed. Put a question mark beside any answer you guessed, and check those first in the answer key.

Sections A-J have definite answers. Sections K-L are productive tasks.

## A. Silent letters

Is the **final consonant** pronounced? Answer yes or no, then say the word aloud.

1. `petit`
2. `avec`
3. `bonjour`
4. `vous`
5. `neuf`
6. `parler`
7. `avril`
8. `grand`

:::answer
| # | Word | Pronounced? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `petit` | No | Most final consonants are silent. |
| 2 | `avec` | Yes | Final **c** is one of the CaReFuL letters. |
| 3 | `bonjour` | Yes | Final **r** is one of the CaReFuL letters. |
| 4 | `vous` | No | Final **s** is normally silent. |
| 5 | `neuf` | Yes | Final **f** is one of the CaReFuL letters. |
| 6 | `parler` | No | The infinitive ending **-er** is a common exception; the final **r** is not pronounced. |
| 7 | `avril` | Yes | Final **l** is one of the CaReFuL letters. |
| 8 | `grand` | No | Final **d** is silent, and the vowel before it is nasal. |

The trap is trusting the spelling too much. In French, a written final consonant is often only a spelling clue, not a sound to pronounce.
:::


## B. `u` versus `ou`

In each pair, which word contains the "oo" sound?

1. `tu` / `tout`
2. `rue` / `roue`
3. `vu` / `vous`
4. `sur` / `sourd`

Say both words in each pair aloud before answering.

:::answer
The **oo** sound is in the second word each time.

1. `tout` — `tu` has the French rounded **u** sound; `tout` has **ou**, the easy **oo** sound.
2. `roue` — `rue` has **u**; `roue` has **ou**.
3. `vous` — `vu` has **u**; `vous` has **ou**.
4. `sourd` — `sur` has **u**; `sourd` has **ou**.

If you cannot hear the difference yet, mark the item for sound practice rather than treating it as a vocabulary error. The mouth position is the rule: French **u** starts from an **ee** tongue position with rounded lips.
:::


## C. Nasal vowels

Does the word contain a nasal vowel? Answer nasal or not nasal, and give the reason.

1. `bon`
2. `bonne`
3. `américain`
4. `américaine`
5. `téléphone`
6. `combien`
7. `un`
8. `une`

:::answer
| # | Word | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `bon` | Nasal | A vowel is followed by **n** with no vowel after it. |
| 2 | `bonne` | Not nasal | The **n** is doubled, so it is pronounced as a consonant. |
| 3 | `américain` | Nasal | The ending **ain** is nasal. |
| 4 | `américaine` | Not nasal | A silent **-e** follows, so the **n** is pronounced. |
| 5 | `téléphone` | Not nasal | A vowel follows the **n**. |
| 6 | `combien` | Nasal twice | Both **om** and **ien** are nasal. |
| 7 | `un` | Nasal | A vowel is followed by **n** with nothing after it. |
| 8 | `une` | Not nasal | A vowel sound follows, so the **n** is pronounced. |

Items 1/2, 3/4, and 7/8 show why gender can change pronunciation. The feminine form often stops the vowel from being nasal.
:::


## D. Elision

Combine the words correctly.

1. `je` + `ai`
2. `le` + `hôtel`
3. `ne` + `est` + `pas`
4. `de` + `eau`
5. `que` + `elle`
6. `la` + `école`

:::answer
1. `j'ai` — `je` loses its vowel before `ai`. Elision is required, not optional.
2. `l'hôtel` — `h` is silent, so `hôtel` behaves like it begins with a vowel sound.
3. `n'est pas` — `ne` becomes `n'` before `est`; `pas` stays after the verb.
4. `d'eau` — `de` becomes `d'` before `eau`.
5. `qu'elle` — `que` becomes `qu'` before `elle`.
6. `l'école` — `la` becomes `l'` before `école`.

The trap is leaving both vowels written out. Forms such as **je ai** or **la école** are not slow French; they are wrong spelling.
:::


## E. Liaison

Mark where two words link, and give the **linking sound**. If there is no liaison, say so and explain why.

1. `les amis`
2. `vous avez`
3. `un grand arbre`
4. `mon ami`
5. `un livre et un stylo`
6. `nous allons`

:::answer
| # | Phrase | Liaison? | Linking sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `les amis` | Yes | Final **s** becomes a **z** sound. |
| 2 | `vous avez` | Yes | Final **s** becomes a **z** sound. |
| 3 | `un grand arbre` | Yes | Final **d** becomes a **t** sound. |
| 4 | `mon ami` | Yes | Final **n** links into the vowel. |
| 5 | `un livre et un stylo` | No | Liaison is never made after `et`; `stylo` also begins with a consonant. |
| 6 | `nous allons` | Yes | Final **s** becomes a **z** sound. |

Do not wake up every silent consonant. Liaison happens in specific environments, and `et` is the first hard no to memorize.
:::


## F. Consonant sounds

Give the sound of the letter or letters in bold type.

1. `ça` — the **ç**
2. `le garçon` — the **ç**
3. `la chaise` — the **ch**
4. `qui` — the **qu**
5. `la montagne` — the **gn**
6. `la maison` — the **s**
7. `le poisson` — the **ss**
8. `je` — the **j**

:::answer
1. `ça` — the **ç** sounds like **s**; the cedilla forces the soft sound before **a**.
2. `le garçon` — the **ç** again sounds like **s**, this time before **o**.
3. `la chaise` — **ch** sounds like **sh**.
4. `qui` — **qu** sounds like **k** only; the **u** is not a separate sound.
5. `la montagne` — **gn** sounds like **ny**, as in canyon.
6. `la maison` — single **s** between vowels sounds like **z**.
7. `le poisson` — doubled **ss** sounds like **s**.
8. `je` — **j** sounds like the **s** in measure.

The important minimal pair is `le poisson` versus `le poison`: doubled **ss** keeps the **s** sound, but one **s** between vowels becomes **z**.
:::


## G. `tu` or `vous`?

Choose the correct form of address.

1. Your closest friend.
2. A waiter you have just met.
3. Two children together.
4. Your grandmother.
5. A new manager at your workplace.
6. Your younger brother.

:::answer
1. `tu` — one close friend takes the familiar form.
2. `vous` — a waiter you have just met takes polite `vous`.
3. `vous` — any group takes `vous`, even a group of children.
4. `tu` — a family member normally takes `tu`.
5. `vous` — a new manager is a person in authority whom you do not know well.
6. `tu` — a younger brother is family and one person.

Item 3 is the common trap. `vous` is both formal singular and ordinary plural, so two people always require `vous`.
:::


## H. What would you say?

Give one appropriate French sentence for each situation.

1. You walk into a bakery at ten in the morning.
2. You want to order a coffee politely.
3. Someone has just thanked you.
4. You did not understand what a person said.
5. You want the person to speak more slowly.
6. You are leaving a shop at midday.

:::answer
Answers vary, but each response must be polite and appropriate to the situation.

1. `Bonjour.` — in a bakery, greet first. Starting directly with the order sounds abrupt.
2. `Un café, s'il vous plaît.` — the request needs `s'il vous plaît`; `Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.` is also correct.
3. `De rien.` — a standard reply to thanks. `Je vous en prie.` is also correct but more formal.
4. `Je ne comprends pas.` — this directly says you did not understand. `Pardon ?` is also usable.
5. `Plus lentement, s'il vous plaît.` — a clear survival phrase for asking someone to slow down.
6. `Au revoir, bonne journée !` — a normal daytime farewell in a shop.

Mark yourself wrong if the French is understandable but socially wrong, such as ordering without `Bonjour` or asking a stranger with `tu`.
:::


## I. Numbers

Write each number in French.

1. 3
2. 8
3. 12
4. 15
5. 17
6. 20

:::answer
1. `trois` — memorize the small numbers as complete sound chunks.
2. `huit` — the final consonant is pronounced when the number stands alone.
3. `douze` — do not confuse it with `deux`; they are different words.
4. `quinze` — the **qu** makes a **k** sound.
5. `dix-sept` — `sept` keeps the **t** sound but not the **p**.
6. `vingt` — the final **t** is silent when the word stands alone.

The spelling trap is that numbers do not all pronounce final letters the same way. Learn each number aloud, not just visually.
:::


## J. Find and fix the error

Each sentence contains one mistake a beginner commonly makes. Rewrite it correctly and name the rule.

1. `Je habite à Paris.`
2. `Je suis vingt ans.`
3. You arrive at a friend's home at 8 p.m. and say: `Bonne nuit !`
4. In a café you say: `Je veux un café.`
5. You ask a stranger on the street: `Tu parles anglais ?`

:::answer
1. `J'habite à Paris.` — `je` must become `j'` before the silent **h** of `habite`.
2. `J'ai vingt ans.` — French uses `avoir` for age, literally to have years. Chapter 2 explains the rule fully.
3. `Bonsoir !` — `Bonne nuit` is for going to bed, not arriving at someone's home in the evening.
4. `Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.` — `Je veux` sounds blunt in a café; soften the request and add `s'il vous plaît`.
5. `Parlez-vous anglais ?` or `Vous parlez anglais ?` — use `vous` with a stranger. The `tu` version is too familiar here.

For each correction, name the rule you fixed. A corrected sentence without the rule is only half learned.
:::


## K. Production

1. Write a five-line self-introduction: name, age, nationality, city, and language level. Memorize it and say it aloud without reading.
2. Write the exact four lines you would say in a bakery: greeting, request, thanks, farewell.
3. Choose five phrases from `survival-phrases.md` that you personally will need this week. Write them out and use each one aloud today.

:::answer
These are productive tasks, so answers vary. Use these models and criteria to check yourself.

**Self-introduction model:**

1. `Bonjour.`
2. `Je m'appelle Ana.`
3. `J'ai dix-neuf ans.`
4. `Je suis canadienne et j'habite à Montréal.`
5. `Je parle un peu français.`

A correct self-introduction includes name, age with `J'ai`, nationality with agreement, city with `J'habite à`, and language level.

**Bakery script model:**

1. `Bonjour.`
2. `Un croissant, s'il vous plaît.`
3. `Merci beaucoup.`
4. `Au revoir, bonne journée !`

It is wrong if it does not open with `Bonjour` or if the request omits `s'il vous plaît`.

For your five survival phrases, answers vary. Choose phrases you can actually use this week, copy accents and apostrophes exactly, and say each one aloud until it is a usable chunk rather than a word-by-word translation.
:::


## L. Dictation and oral recall

1. **Dictation.** Use text-to-speech on the website to hear six sentences from the "Sentence usage practice" sections of `lessons.md`. Write what you hear without looking. Correct in two passes: first the sounds you missed, then the silent letters and accents you left out.
2. **Read aloud.** Read the "Connected reading: mon premier jour" passage aloud twice. Record the second attempt and check it for even syllable rhythm.
3. **Oral recall.** Explain aloud, in simple English, and then give one French example from memory:
   - which final consonants are usually pronounced;
   - what makes a vowel nasal;
   - the difference between elision and liaison;
   - when to use `vous` instead of `tu`.

:::answer
These tasks do not have one hidden script, but they do have clear standards.

**Dictation:** score each sentence in three passes: words, accents, and silent letters you wrote but did not pronounce. The silent-letter pass matters because French spelling often records information your ear cannot hear.

**Read aloud:** judge rhythm first. Even syllable rhythm is more important at this stage than a perfect French `r`.

**Oral recall model answers:**

- **Final consonants:** most are silent, but **c**, **r**, **f**, and **l** are often pronounced. The infinitive ending **-er** is a common exception. Example: `avec`.
- **Nasal vowels:** a vowel followed by **n** or **m** is nasal when no vowel follows and the consonant is not doubled. Example: `bon`.
- **Elision versus liaison:** elision removes a vowel and writes an apostrophe, as in `j'ai`; liaison wakes up a silent final consonant before a vowel sound, as in `les amis`.
- **`vous`:** use it with strangers, older people, people in authority, and any group. Example: `Comment allez-vous ?`

If you can state the rule but cannot give a French example, redo the recall tomorrow. Production is the goal.
:::
